# INTRODUCTORY QUESTIONS

## What is this contract?

This contract operates alongside any general terms and conditions on your
website (“Website Terms”), and governs how your website content may be accessed
and used. It provides for a fee to be paid for each discrete “Product” (such as
an article, image, video or audio file) on the site which is accessed. We refer
to this below as the “Access Fee”.

You must decide your own Access Fee and state it in your Website Terms (see “How
do I apply this to my Website?” below). The extent to which you differentiate –
or don’t differentiate – the Fee is left to your discretion. I.e. you could
choose to demand one standard Fee per Product, or you could choose to demand a
different Fee for each type of Product. Either way, the selected amount should
be reasonable and proportionate, rather than excessive, as you must be able to
justify it in the event that you need to bring a debt claim for non-payment. In
practice, the Fee will typically reflect the market in which you operate.

The Access Fee is incurred each time a Product is accessed. It is waived only
where the access falls strictly within Licensed Access (Non-Commercial Use or
Search Indexing) and all the conditions of the Contract are met. Where the
access falls outside Licensed Access, it is deemed Unauthorised Access and the
Access Fee becomes immediately due and payable.

The effect of this is that, where your website is accessed by a consumer for
their own personal use or by a bot for the purpose of indexing your site on a
Search Engine Results Page, the Fee is waived. Where the site is accessed for
scraping, bulk extraction, dataset creation, or any AI-related use, the Fee
remains due and payable.

## Why does it exist?

This contract exists to draw a clear boundary between what is permitted and what
requires permission.

It allows standard search engine indexing so that your website can be
discovered, while making clear that activities such as AI training, dataset
creation, bulk scraping, or other data-driven reuse are not included in that
permission.

Historically, these uses have often been treated as overlapping or implicit.
This contract removes that ambiguity by expressly separating ordinary search
indexing from AI and data-extraction activities and treating the latter as
controlled uses that require explicit authorisation.

## Who does it apply to?

This contract applies to any person, entity, or system that accesses or
interacts with your website or its content, regardless of the method or
technology used.

It covers access by:

- individual human users,
- companies and other organisations,
- search engines,
- automated crawlers and bots, and
- Artificial Intelligence Systems or machine-driven agents.

It applies equally whether access is:

- manual, such as a person visiting pages through a browser, or
- automated, such as bots, scrapers, crawlers, indexing tools, or AI-driven
  systems.

The intention is that there are no gaps or grey areas. If a person, company,
program, or system in any way accesses, retrieves, processes, or interacts with
your website or its content, that access falls within the scope of this contract
and is governed by its terms.

## How does it work (step by step)?

Think of it as working in layers — both legal and technical — at the same time:

### Legal layer (the contract itself)

The contract is made visible on the website, incorporated into your Website
Terms. (Please see “How do I apply this Contract” below for more on how to apply
the contract on your Website.)

The contract clearly states that anyone who accesses or uses the website is
subject to its rules. Acceptance does not require a tick box or signature.
Rather, agreement is formed through conduct, simply by accessing, viewing,
crawling, indexing, or otherwise interacting with the website or its content.
This ensures that the terms apply automatically.

### Technical layer (machine-readable signals)

Alongside the legal contract,
the website uses technical, machine-readable links to the contract to communicate
permissions and restrictions to automated systems.

These may include:

- robots.txt files,
- HTTP headers, and
- other standard machine-readable signals.

These signals tell automated systems — such as crawlers, bots, and AI systems —
the location of the contract which defines what they are allowed to do and what
they are not allowed to do when accessing the website. As many websites use the
same location for the Contract it is machine-readable and can be readily
identified across many websites.

### Alignment between the two

The legal terms and the technical instructions are intentionally designed to say
the same thing:

- Search engines are permitted to crawl content strictly for search indexing and
  discovery;
- AI systems and data-harvesting tools are not permitted to ingest content for
  training, datasets, or other AI-related uses without gaining explicit
  additional permission.

Because the legal rules and technical signals are aligned, there is no ambiguity
about permitted use. This significantly strengthens enforcement, as a user or
system has been put on notice in more than one way.

## Works alongside other website policies

This contract does not replace any existing website policies. Instead, it
operates alongside them, each addressing a different aspect of how the website
is run and used.

In the same way as:

- cookie policies explain how cookies and tracking technologies are used,
- privacy notices explain how personal data is handled, and
- general terms of use set out the overall rules for using the site,

this contract focuses specifically on how website content may be accessed,
indexed, and reused, particularly by automated systems and AI-driven
technologies.

## CONTRACT TERMS

### Is this Contract binding if the accessing party never expressly agrees to it?

**Answer:**

Short answer:

Yes. The Contract still applies.

Longer explanation:

When someone visits or uses the website after being clearly told there is a
Contract governing access, the law generally treats that as acceptance. This
approach is widely used across the web. You do not need a pop up or tick box for
the rules to apply. What matters is that you have taken reasonable steps to make
sure that the Contract and the access fee amount is brought to the attention of
the accessing party.

Under Version 3 the Access Fee is no longer a default £500 fee, so you will need
to independently set the amount in your Website Terms and Conditions.

This means following the above instructions, and making sure that:

1. the Contract is incorporated into the Website Terms and Conditions;
2. you have independently chosen appropriate amount(s) for the Access Fee per
   product, and included the relevant clause in your Website Terms and
   Conditions;
3. the lines set out above are added to the robots.txt file; and
4. screenshots are taken to evidence the implementation.

Additionally, to ensure a belt-and-braces approach has been adopted in
implementing the Contract, it is advisable to send letters to the legal heads of
each major bot owner, to evidence that the position has been properly
communicated.

This may also involve making it clear, when sending an invoice, that the
recipient is subject to the Contract, and providing a link to the Contract and
an explanation of where and how it has been incorporated.

### How will AI companies and other third parties know that the Contract applies to my Website?

**Answer:**

The Contract is communicated through several channels at the same time, so that
both individuals and automated systems are put on notice:

- robots.txt: the lines set out in step 2 of “How do I apply this to my
  Website?” tell every crawler, through the “tdl” (Terms Document Locator)
  field, where the Contract is located. robots.txt is the recognised place where
  automated systems look for a website’s instructions, and the High Court has
  recognised its use to control indexing as standard and well-accepted practice
  among webmasters.
- Website Terms and Conditions: the wording at step 3 incorporates the Contract
  into your human-readable website terms.
- Website notices: a footer link or on-page notice referring to the Contract.
  For maximum notice, this might take the form of a banner ad on your site.
- Letters to crawler/AI operators: you should also write to the operators of the
  major web crawlers, search engines and AI large language models, informing
  them that your Website is governed by the Contract, providing a link to the
  version of the Contract that applies, and stating the Access Fee set out in
  your Website Terms and Conditions.
- MOW will also send its own letters to the main AI Companies, including Meta,
  Google, OpenAI, Anthropic, X and Microsoft. If you bring a claim against one
  of these companies, you can rely on the letter sent by MOW as evidence, and
  should reference it in your letter before claim. However, you will still need
  to send the above notification letter. MOW will make these available on their
  website after they have been sent.

Sending a letter is not a legal requirement for the Contract to apply, but it
removes any argument that the operator was unaware of the Contract and
strengthens your position if you later need to enforce it. The letter should be
addressed to the legal department or registered office of the relevant company.

Keep a copy of each letter together with proof of sending and delivery (for
example, an email delivery receipt or recorded delivery confirmation). Any
access by that operator’s crawlers after receipt of the letter will have taken
place with clear notice of the Contract.

*LEGAL NOTE*

*Incorporation turns on whether the Website Operator has done what is reasonably
sufficient to bring the terms to the attention of the Accessing Party, not on
whether the terms were actually read (per Parker-Grennan v Camelot UK Lotteries
Ltd [2024] EWCA Civ 185). The more onerous or unusual the terms, the more notice
that is required. Notice through several channels (robots.txt, website terms,
website notices and direct letters) gives the strongest position in this
regard.*

*The Website Operator bears the burden of proving incorporation.
Contemporaneous, dated evidence of each notice channel is far more persuasive

**Isn’t this just trying to contract around copyright limits (e.g. fair dealing

**Answer:** The Contract doesn’t replace copyright law. Instead, it sets

### Does allowing search engine indexing weaken the restriction on AI use?

**Answer:** No. Unlike other available mechanisms, the Contract does not
outright prohibit all access to the website. It distinguishes between permitted
access for Search Indexing and other uses. Search engines are allowed to look at
pages so they can point visitors to the original site. What they are not allowed
to do is reuse the content for AI training, summaries, or datasets. Permission
is given for a narrow purpose only.

Why this may come up: The distinction between search indexing and AI use is
increasingly blurred (particularly with large platforms), so this is a likely
point of confusion.

### How does this interact with existing “free-to-index” norms?

**Answer:** It builds on them for search. Free indexing is preserved as a
baseline. The Contract just clarifies that AI and reuse are separate activities
requiring permission. The Contract preserves standard search indexing as a
baseline, while making clear that other forms of reuse—particularly AI-related
uses—are separate and require express permission which the Website Operator can
negotiate with the AI company separately to the Contract.

## SCRAPING, CRAWLING & TECHNICAL CONTROL

### How do you prove that scraping or AI training has taken place?

**Answer:** Content can carry hidden identifiers or patterns that show up later
inside AI outputs or datasets. If those indicators appear, and there’s no
permission on record, Unlicensed Access has likely happened.

Some Accessing Parties might include links to the original source of results
they display. Such links would prove Unlicensed Access has happened.

Further some Accessing Parties may identify themselves using IP addresses and
User-Agent strings which can be recorded in log files to prove that access took
place.

The Contract also allows the website owner to ask the organisation involved to
explain how the content was collected and used.

Why this may come up: Enforcement against AI systems is often seen as
evidentially difficult, so this is likely to be a key practical concern.

### How should publishers practically evidence repeated infringement at scale?

**Answer:** Evidence can be built by demonstrating patterns, not perfection
through keyword tracking, systematic sampling of AI outputs, and screen captures
showing replication of content. Courts and regulators don’t expect every
instance to be captured. What matters is showing that misuse is happening
repeatedly or systematically. For example, through repeated AI outputs that
closely resemble the Website Content.

*LEGAL NOTE*

*The idea of “pattern evidence” is important.*

*Avoid language suggesting every instance must be proven — that raises the bar
unnecessarily.*

### How do we address AI companies relying on third-party scrapers?

**Answer:**

The short answer is that it depends on the cause of action pursued under the
Contract and the relationship between the third-party scraper and the party that
eventually holds the content.

The debt action available under the Contract is designed to be enforced against
the party that accessed the Website and scraped the Website Content. This would
mean that if the AI Company bought the content from the reseller, then the party
that accessed the Website and scraped the content would be responsible for the
payment of the Access Fee, rather than the AI Company that then bought the
scraped content. You would not be able to bring the claim against the AI
Company. It may be possible to argue that the AI Company is responsible for the
scrape, but that would require the AI Company actively controlling the scraper.

However, if you were advancing an Intellectual Property Claim under the
Contract, you may be able to bring the claim directly against the AI Company.

Any AI operator found to be using unlicensed sources is likely to suffer brand
embarrassment as well as significant financial costs. This acts as a commercial
incentive to cease such practices.

### What infrastructure is needed to monitor infringement?

**Answer:** Monitoring is expected to combine internal checks and
specialist tools. Publishers don’t need complex infrastructure. Simple
monitoring, combined with third party services where needed, is usually
sufficient. External services, for which there is a growing market for
third-party tools that specialise in detecting and evidencing AI-related misuse
can be used – indeed, such tools may be very helpful evidentially.

### Can token-based or cryptographic systems realistically track content use?

**Answer:** Yes, especially together. Tokenisation and provenance systems could
allow content to be traced across reuse contexts. No single system is perfect,
but combined technical markers make it much easier to detect content reuse and
support enforcement where problems arise.

### How is the Contract machine-readable, and what role do Terms Document Locators (TDLs) play?

**Answer:**

“Machine-readable” means that an automated system, such as a crawler or AI
agent, can locate and identify the terms that apply to a website without human
intervention. The Contract is made machine-readable in three ways.

First, robots.txt. A robots.txt file is a plain text file at the root of a
website (yourdomain.com/robots.txt) which tells crawlers and other automated
agents which parts of the website they may access. The core robots.txt standard
is published by the Internet Engineering Task Force (IETF) as RFC 9309, and
signatories to the EU’s General-Purpose AI Code of Practice commit to using
crawlers that read and follow robots.txt instructions. robots.txt operates as
part of a mosaic of conventions and standards that can be extended over time, as
happened with sitemaps.

Second, the “tdl” field. TDL stands for Terms Document Locator. The line
beginning “tdl:” in your robots.txt file tells any crawler reading the file
where the Contract governing access is located. The “tdl” field is one of
several complementary fields that can be placed in a robots.txt file alongside
the standard “User-agent”, “Allow” and “Disallow” directives.

Third, fixed, version-controlled URLs. Each version of the Contract is hosted by
MOW at a fixed URL whose content never changes. Because many websites point to
the same URL, an automated system can recognise the Contract across all of those
websites and know exactly which terms apply. The Contract can also be linked
from the HTML \<head\> of each webpage (see “Do changes need to be made to
HTML?” below) and from HTTP response headers.

The more widely the “tdl” field and the Contract are adopted, the more readily
AI systems will recognise and act on them, and the harder it becomes for an
Accessing Party to argue that it was unaware of the terms. Publishers are
encouraged to support formal recognition of the “tdl” field at the IETF and in
other standards forums.

Other mechanisms, such as Really Simple Licensing (“RSL”), also use robots.txt
as a signpost, pointing to a document that sets out the website’s licensing
preferences and how to obtain a licence. However, RSL does not itself contain
enforcement provisions. The Contract goes further: it is a binding contract with
its own enforcement mechanisms, including the right to recover the Access Fee as
a debt.

*LEGAL NOTE*

*Keep “machine-readable legal restrictions” language — it connects technical
signals to legal intent.*

*For Website Operators subject to EU law, an express, machine-readable
reservation of rights is relevant to the text and data mining opt-out in Article
4(3) of Directive (EU) 2019/790 and to the obligations of general-purpose AI
model providers under Article 53(1)(c) of the EU AI Act. The robots.txt and
“tdl” implementation is intended to support that reservation as well as
incorporation under English contract law.*

### Does this Contract prevent accessibility tools or assistive technologies (e.g. screen readers)?

**Answer:** No. The Contract does not restrict accessibility or assistive
technologies. Tools such as screen readers, text-to-speech systems, and other
accessibility agents used to help people with disabilities access the website
are permitted. The Contract targets bulk reuse and secondary exploitation of
content, not user-facing non-commercial access (Clause 4.4 and the definition of
“Non-Commercial Use” in Annex A).

**Why this may come up:**  
Accessibility advocates and regulators are rightly concerned about anything that
could chill or impair access for people with disabilities. This question helps
make clear that the Contract targets misuse, not accessibility.

**Can websites covered by this Contract be used for academic and research
purposes?**

**Answer:** Limited non-commercial access (including reading and research) is
permitted. However, activities such as scraping, dataset creation, or AI
training — even for research — require separate permission.

### Do changes need to be made to HTML?

**Answer:**

No. There is no requirement to modify your website’s HTML in order to implement
the Contract.

The primary method of implementation remains through machine-readable signals
such as the robots.txt file, which identifies the applicable Terms Document
Locator (TDL).

However, publishers may choose to additionally include references to the
Contract within the HTML of their webpages. For example, where multiple licences
are used at the following URLs:

```plain
https://m4ow.uk/socw/3.txt
https://example.com/other/tos.txt
```

These can also be included in the HTML \<head\> element via the terms-of-service
link relation, as shown below:

```xml
<head>
<link rel="terms-of-service" href="https://m4ow.uk/socw/3.txt"/>
<link rel="terms-of-service" href="https://example.com/other/tos.txt"/>
</head>
```

This approach is optional and does not replace the need to correctly implement
the robots.txt configuration, but may provide an additional layer of visibility
and consistency across systems.

### Can websites covered by this Contract be used in Common Crawl?

**Answer:**

Only if Common Crawl has entered into a binding agreement ensuring that Website
Content is not used or shared for any restricted purposes under this Contract.
In practice, inclusion in Common Crawl would typically constitute Dataset
creation and downstream distribution, which is prohibited unless expressly
licensed.

### Can websites covered by this Contract be added to the Internet Archive?

**Answer:**

No, unless the Internet Archive (or equivalent service) has entered into an
express written agreement with the Website Operator permitting such activity. In
the absence of such permission, this is not a permitted use. This is because
such activity involves the systematic copying, storage, and potential
redistribution of Website Content, which falls outside the limited licence
granted under Clause 4 (Licence to Access Website) and is prohibited under
Clause 7 (Prohibition on AI Scraping and Data Mining) and Clause 8 (Database
Rights Protection), and is further restricted by the machine-readable controls
described in Clause 10.

## COMPENSATION & COMMERCIAL USE

### What amount should the Access Fee be?

**Answer:**

There is no standard price or marketplace for valuing content. This Contract
does not seek to apply a market value, but rather simply makes clear that use
beyond search indexing is not free by default. The Contract does not set a
default Access Fee. Each Website Operator must set its own Access Fee, which is
payable for each Product (for example, an article, image, video or audio file)
accessed outside the scope of Licensed Access, and must state the amount in its
own Website Terms and Conditions using the wording at step 3(B) of “How do I
apply this to my Website?”.

Any access fee should be reasonable and capable of objective justification,
taking into account factors such as the costs associated with creating,
maintaining and making content available, together with any other relevant
commercial considerations. The fee should not be set at an excessive or
exorbitant level. What constitutes a reasonable fee will depend on the
particular circumstances of the publisher and the industry in which it operates.
We generally recommend adopting a conservative approach to pricing, as this may
reduce the risk of a counterparty arguing that the fee is unconscionable or
unenforceable as a penalty. Publishers should therefore retain records
demonstrating the basis on which the fee was determined and why it is considered
reasonable.

When setting your Access Fee, you may wish to consider:

- whether to charge a single Access Fee for all Products or different Access
  Fees for different types of Product (for example, written works, images, video
  and audio);
- the license fees you have charged, or been offered, for comparable uses of
  your content, including under any licensing agreements with search engine or
  AI companies;
- the cost of producing the relevant content; and
- publicly available information about licensing agreements between publishers
  and AI developers (for example, the CREATe AI Licence Tracker).

Your Access Fee should be a figure you can explain and justify by reference to
that evidence, and you should keep a record of how you arrived at it. The more
onerous or unusual a term, the more a court will require to have been done to
bring it to the other party’s attention.

Each publisher must set its Access Fee independently. You must not discuss or
agree your Access Fee with other publishers (see “Would a collective enforcement
model be more effective?” below).

If you change your Access Fee, update your Website Terms and Conditions and
archive the updated version (see step 4 of “How do I apply this to my
Website?”). The revised amount will apply to access taking place after the
change.

*LEGAL NOTE*

*The Access Fee is structured as a price for access (a primary obligation)
recoverable as a debt, rather than as damages for breach. A figure that cannot
be justified by reference to value invites arguments that the term is onerous
and was insufficiently notified, or that it operates as a penalty.*

### Would introducing payment expectations undermine free search visibility?

**Answer:**

No. The Contract waives payment for search indexing and non-commercial use.
Payment only applies where companies want to use the content for other purposes,
such as AI training.

If you find your search traffic decreases as an incident of your adoption of the
Contract, this would seemingly be a punitive measure by a company that engages
in both search indexing and AI scraping. See further “Is there a risk of
retaliation from search engines or platforms?” below.

## ENFORCEMENT & LITIGATION

### What happens if a company simply ignores the Contract and operates outside the UK?

**Answer:**

Even if a company is overseas, courts can order intermediaries — like internet
providers or platforms — to stop access or block misuse. These tools are
specifically designed for situations where bad actors are hard to reach
directly.

### Can I bring a claim through Money Claim Online against an Accessing Party based outside England and Wales?

**Answer:**

No. Money Claim Online (“MCOL”) can only be used where each defendant has an
address for service in England and Wales (Practice Direction 7C, paragraph
4(3A)). If the Accessing Party does not have an address for service in England
and Wales, the claim will generally need to be issued in the traditional manner
using a paper claim form (Form N1) through the County Court or, where
appropriate, the Intellectual Property Enterprise Court. Before serving the
claim outside the jurisdiction, you will generally be required to apply for the
Court's permission pursuant to Civil Procedure Rule (CPR) r.6.37. As
applications for service out of the jurisdiction can involve complex procedural
and jurisdictional issues, we recommend seeking independent legal advice before
commencing this process.

Obtaining a judgment is only the first step. If the defendant has no assets in
England and Wales, the judgment will need to be enforced in the country where
its assets are located, under that country’s rules for recognising English
judgments. You should take legal advice before bringing a claim against an
overseas defendant.

### How do I know which company is the right defendant?

**Answer:**

Under the Contract, the Accessing Party is the person or entity accessing the
Website, whether directly or indirectly, and an Unlicensed User includes the
operator, developer, owner, controller or other responsible party directing the
relevant AI system or crawler. The right defendant is therefore the legal entity
that operates or directs the crawler or AI system, which is not always the
company whose brand name appears on the product.

To identify the right defendant:

1. Identify the crawler. Your server logs will record the User-Agent string and
   IP address of each request. Many operators publish the names of their
   crawlers and the IP address ranges they use.
2. Identify the operating entity. The operator’s published terms of service and
   privacy policy usually name the legal entity that provides the service. Check
   Companies House (or the equivalent overseas register) for that entity’s full
   name, registered number and registered address.
3. Consider the group structure. Large technology groups often operate through
   several companies. A UK subsidiary may only provide sales or marketing
   services, while the crawler is operated by an overseas parent company. Naming
   the wrong defendant may result in the claim failing, and you may need to
   discontinue proceedings. Although costs are generally limited on the small
   claims track, you will inevitably lose the court fee you paid.
4. Ask. Under Clauses 4.7 and 11 of the Contract, you may require the Accessing
   Party to disclose how Website Content was accessed and used. Under clauses
   4.7 and 11 of the Contract, you may require the Accessing Party to disclose
   how the Website Content was accessed and used. During the pre-action stage,
   you should also expressly request confirmation that the recipient is the
   correct defendant. If no such clarification is provided, you may proceed on
   the basis that the recipient is the correct defendant, whilst reserving your
   right to seek costs should it later transpire that the recipient knew, or
   ought reasonably to have known, that it was not the correct defendant.

Where content has been obtained by a third-party scraper and then used by an AI
company, it is more likely that the third-party scraper will be the party
against which the debt claim will be enforced, as they are the ones that access
the website (see “How do we address AI companies relying on third-party
scrapers?” above).

### Is it viable to bring multiple small claims for repeated infringements?

**Answer:**

While technically possible, this is usually inefficient. Straightforward claims
may begin in lower courts for speed and cost efficiency, but complex or
high-value disputes may move to higher courts. While small claims are available,
bringing dozens or hundreds of them is inefficient. A better approach is to
group evidence and use a handful of cases to show widespread misuse. A mixed
strategy is expected, with at least one strong case used to establish precedent.
The Contract allows aggregation of multiple Unlicensed Access Events into a
single claim, and the Website Operator may choose the most efficient strategy,
including issuing aggregated claims or multiple proceedings where appropriate.

Publishers should also be aware that the court has power to consolidate separate
claims, or to order that they be tried together (CPR 3.1(2)(g) and (h)). If a
publisher issues a series of small claims against the same defendant arising
from the same conduct, the court may, and is likely to, combine them into a
single claim, which will then be allocated to the track that reflects its total
value. Claims above £10,000 will generally fall outside the small claims track,
with significantly different costs consequences (see “Are there costs risks in
bringing a small claim?” below).

### What is the most effective litigation pathway if cases escalate?

**Answer:**

It depends on the scale and seriousness. Smaller disputes can be handled quickly
and cheaply via the UK Small Claims courts and Money Claim Online. Bigger or
more complex cases may need higher courts. Often, one strong case sets the tone
and resolves many others without further litigation.

**How do I make a claim?**

The Contract provides a structured framework for bringing claims as a debt after
issuing the invoice, including the ability to recover sums due through England
and Wales small claims track via Money Claim Online (where the defendant has an
address for service in England and Wales: see “Can I bring a claim through Money
Claim Online against an Accessing Party based outside England and Wales?”
above). We have prepared a "Guide to Small Claims via Money Claims Online",
which will guide you through the practical steps such as issuing invoices,
sending a Letter Before Claim, and using the streamlined procedure envisioned by
using the small claims track where appropriate. The approach is designed to
enable efficient and proportionate enforcement, including by publishers without
extensive litigation resources. (For more detail, please see [the Small Claims
Guidance](Guide%20to%20Small%20Claims%20via%20Money%20Claims%20Online.pdf).)

### Are there costs risks in bringing a small claim?

**Answer:**

The costs risks on the small claims track are limited, but they are not nil.

Claims with a value of up to £10,000 are normally allocated to the small claims
track. On that track, the general rule is that the losing party does not pay the
winning party’s legal costs (CPR 27.14). The amounts recoverable from the other
side are limited to items such as court fees, the fixed costs of issuing the
claim, and limited expenses for witnesses and experts. This means that:

- if you win, you will usually recover the debt, interest and court fees, but
  not the cost of any legal representation; and
- if you lose, you will usually bear only your own court fees and costs, and
  will not have to pay the defendant’s legal costs.

There are two important exceptions:

1. Unreasonable behaviour. The court can order a party that has behaved
   unreasonably to pay the other side’s costs (CPR 27.14(2)(g)). This applies to
   claimants as well as defendants, so publishers should follow the pre-action
   steps, including sending a Letter Before Claim, and should not pursue claims
   they cannot support with evidence.
2. Claims above the small claims limit. If the claim exceeds £10,000, whether on
   issue or because the court consolidates several claims (see “Is it viable to
   bring multiple small claims for repeated infringements?” above), it will
   generally be allocated to the fast track, intermediate track or multi-track.
   On those tracks, the losing party will generally be ordered to pay the
   winning party’s costs, either as fixed recoverable costs or as assessed
   costs, so the costs exposure is considerably greater.

You should also budget for court fees (which increase with the value of the
claim), any hearing fee, and the cost of enforcing a judgment if the defendant
does not pay.

### What other enforcement mechanisms are available under the Contract?

**Answer:**

In addition to recovering the Access Fee as a debt (Clause 12), the Contract
reserves a range of other rights and remedies. These operate cumulatively, so
the Website Operator does not have to choose between them or exhaust one before
pursuing another (Clauses 2.10, 12.8 and 13.6).

Whether any particular remedy is granted is a matter for the court and will
depend on the facts of each case. Many of these remedies, in particular
injunctions, website-blocking orders and orders relating to AI models, require
High Court proceedings and are significantly more costly than a small claim.
Publishers should take legal advice before pursuing them.

### Would a collective enforcement model be more effective?

**Answer:**

Whilst collective action reduces costs, avoids duplication, and increases
leverage especially against large platforms, and increases pressure on
non-compliant actors, each publisher must act in accordance with its own
independent commercial interests and must not coordinate with its competitors on
the prices that it may choose to charge for its products. Were publishers to act
collectively, this may be construed as an illegal price-fixing cartel under the
Competition Act 1998.

The Contract provides a convenient baseline set of terms to enable
enforceability against AI scraping given the context of widespread taking
without payment or permission. It has been created by a non-profit as support to
the industry and is aligned with the CMA’s enforcement mechanisms that have been
imposed on Google with relation to its investigation of Google under the Digital
Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 and the obligations imposed in the
CMA’s Publisher Conduct Requirement final decision and Fair Ranking Conduct
Requirement final decision.

### Should enforcement go through regulators rather than courts?

**Answer:**

Regulatory routes complement litigation. Regulators are useful for systemic
issues, while courts remain the fastest way to stop specific misuse. However,
litigation remains an important and immediate enforcement mechanism.

### Is there a risk of retaliation from search engines or platforms?

**Answer:**

There is some risk, but large-scale withdrawal of indexing is considered
unlikely due to commercial, regulatory, and competitive pressures. Search
engines need high quality content. Widespread adoption of this Contract, or
similar policies, reduces the incentive to retaliate.

On 17 July 2026, the CMA imposed a Fair Ranking Conduct Requirement on Google,
under the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024, as part of its
Strategic Market Status investigation into Google's search services. Section 4
requires Google to rank organic search results using objective,
non-discriminatory criteria, meaning it cannot penalise a publisher for opting
out of AI Overviews or AI Mode, or for exercising contractual or statutory
rights against Google (such as a debt recovery claim). If Google retaliates by
downranking, that's a breach the CMA can investigate and remedy with an
enforcement order forcing Google to comply and reverse the damage.

## CONTRACT ECOSYSTEM AND ADOPTION

### What is the role of a central coordinating body (e.g. Movement for an Open Web)?

**Answer:**

Primarily to coordinate policy documents, not control. Its role would be to
support standards, tools, and consistency, not to take content ownership or run
lawsuits. The model is intended to remain decentralised, with publishers
retaining full control.

### Will adoption be standardised across publishers and jurisdictions?

**Answer:**

Full standardisation is unlikely in the short term. Different markets will move
at different speeds, but shared principles are likely to emerge.
